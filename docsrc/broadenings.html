Spectral broadenings

Overview

EPR spectra do not consist of a discrete set of infinitely sharp lines. Lines are broadened by dynamic effects (relaxation, tumbling, chemical exchange) or static effects (orientational disorder, unresolved hyperfine or dipolar splittings, distributions in magnetic properties such as g, A, and D values, magnetic-field inhomogeneity).

EasySpin takes orientational disorder into account explicitly for solid-state spectra. You can include some forms of additional broadening in most spectral simulations (solid-state cw EPR with pepper, liquid EPR with garlic, ENDOR with salt). In addition, any static broadening model can be implemented using explicit averaging.

This page covers the following broadening models:

  • Isotropic convolutional broadening (lw, lwpp, lwEndor)
  • Anisotropic residual line width (HStrain)
  • Strains (StrainPars, StrainFWHM, StrainCorr, StrainModes)
  • Explicit averaging over distributions

The first three are built into EasySpin. Convolutional broadening is isotropic: every line in the spectrum gets the same width and shape. The anisotropic residual line width and strains are anisotropic: each resonance line gets its own Gaussian width, which depends on the orientation of the spin center relative to the magnetic field and, for strains, also on the transition. Explicit averaging is not built in. You write a loop over a parameter distribution yourself, which works for any distribution and any parameter.

The broadenings are given in fields of the spin system structure, which contains the spin system and all associated spin Hamiltonian parameters. Not all types of broadenings are supported by all simulation functions. For instance, broadenings are treated differently in the simulation of slow-motion cw EPR spectra using chili. See the documentation of chili.

All broadenings are understood to be FWHM (full width at half maximum) or PP (peak-to-peak), independent of the simulation function, the line shape or the detection harmonic. For the conversion to and from peak-to-peak line widths, see the reference page on line shapes.

Use either convolutional or anisotropic broadening (HStrain and strains), but not both at the same time.

Isotropic convolutional broadening

This type of broadening is applied by convolving the final simulated field-swept or frequency-swept stick spectrum with a single Gaussian, Lorentzian or Voigtian line shape. Therefore, it applies the same line width and line shape to each transition and orientation. It is the simplest broadening model and is not based on any physical cause of the broadening, which is why it is often called "phenomenological". It can be used to visually adjust the broadening of a simulated spectrum to match that of an experimental one.

Isotropic convolutional broadening should only be used for S=1/2 systems with very small g anisotropy, and for S>1/2 systems with small zero-field splittings. For systems with substantial g anisotropy, or systems with higher electron spin and substantial zero-field splitting, it will lead to incorrect results. The reason is that in those cases, distributions in g and D are almost always dominant, and a simple convolutional broadening is inappropriate.

The following fields in the spin system structure specify convolutional broadening:

lwpp
Line width for isotropic broadening (PP, peak-to-peak), used for convolution of a field-swept or frequency-swept liquid or solid-state cw EPR spectrum. Peak-to-peak refers to the horizontal distance between the maximum and the minimum of a first-derivative line shape.

For field-swept spectra, the unit of lwpp is mT. For frequency-swept spectra, the unit is MHz.

  • 1 element: Gaussian
  • 2 elements: [Gaussian Lorentzian]
Sys.lwpp = 10;       % Gaussian broadening
Sys.lwpp = [0 12];   % Lorentzian broadening
Sys.lwpp = [10 12];  % Voigtian broadening (Gaussian + Lorentzian)

For conversion between FWHM and PP line widths, see the reference page on line shapes.

lw
Same as lwpp, except that the numbers are assumed to indicate the full width at half maximum (FWHM) instead of the peak-to-peak (PP) width. For conversion between FWHM and PP line widths, see the reference page on line shapes.
lwEndor
Line widths (FWHM, Gaussian and Lorentzian) for convolutional broadening of ENDOR spectra. Usage is the same as lw. For lwEndor, no peak-to-peak analogue is available. See the page on line shapes for conversion formulas.
Anisotropic residual line width

In solid-state spectra, the line width often depends on the orientation of the spin center relative to the magnetic field. To model broadening from unresolved hyperfine or dipolar splittings and other nonspecific effects, use HStrain. For broadening from distributions of specific spin Hamiltonian parameters, use strains instead. HStrain and strains can be used together. Then, for each orientation and transition, EasySpin adds a Gaussian line whose width combines both contributions.

HStrain
[FWHM_x FWHM_y FWHM_z]
Anisotropic residual line width (full width at half maximum, FWHM), in MHz, describing broadening due to unresolved hyperfine couplings or other transition-independent effects. The three components are the Gaussian line widths in the x, y and z direction of the molecular frame. 
Sys.HStrain = [10 10 10];        % 10 MHz Gaussian FWHM broadening in all directions
Sys.HStrain = [10 10 50];        % broadening along the molecular z axis larger than along x and y

Strains

A strain is a distribution of spin Hamiltonian parameters due to small structural variations among the paramagnetic centers in a sample. EasySpin models strains as Gaussian distributions of one or more parameters around the values given in the spin system structure. The distributions of different parameters can be correlated. For example, the distributions of gx, gy and gz are often correlated, since they depend on the same structural variation. Another example is the correlation between g and A values.

For each orientation and each transition, EasySpin computes how fast the transition frequency changes with each strained parameter, and from this the (frequency-domain) width of the resulting Gaussian line. If a parameter has a large effect on the transition frequency, its distribution gives a broad line. If it has no effect, it gives no broadening. Correlated parameters can broaden or narrow the lines, depending on whether their effects add or cancel. For field sweeps, the frequency-domain width is converted to a field width. If HStrain is given as well, the two widths are combined as the square root of the sum of their squares.

As a consequence, some lines can have zero width. For example, if only gz is strained, the lines with the magnetic field perpendicular to the z axis do not depend on gz and are not broadened at all. Such lines appear as very sharp spikes in the simulated spectrum. To avoid this, add a small HStrain, which gives every line a minimum width.

Strains are specified with four fields:

StrainPars
cell array of character arrays, e.g. {'g(1)','A(1,3)'}
List of strained parameters. Each entry refers to one element of a spin system field, using MATLAB indexing as for the field itself: 'g(3)' is the third element of Sys.g, and 'A(2,3)' is the element in row 2 and column 3 of Sys.A. A field name alone, e.g. 'D', can be used if the field has only one element. The parameters refer to the fields as you give them. For example, with Sys.D = [D E], 'D(1)' is D and 'D(2)' is E. With axial Sys.g = [gperp gpar], 'g(1)' varies gx and gy together.

The following fields can be strained: g, A, D, Q, ee, J, dip, dvec, nn, sigma, and the Euler angles in gFrame, AFrame, DFrame, QFrame, eeFrame, nnFrame, and sigmaFrame (in radians). For a tensor given as a full 3x3 matrix, the elements cannot be strained. Instead, give the tensor as [xx yy zz xy xz yz], whose elements can be strained. The frame angles of such tensors cannot be strained, since the six elements already include the orientation.

Strains are not possible for nuclei in groups of equivalent nuclei (Sys.n larger than 1). A strained parameter that has no effect on the spectrum gives an error, for example a strain of D for an electron spin 1/2 or of Q for a nucleus with spin 1/2. If a nucleus is given as an element, e.g. 'Cu', the strains of its hyperfine and quadrupole values are scaled for each isotope in the same way as the values themselves. 

Sys.StrainPars = {'g(3)'};                % gz, with Sys.g = [gx gy gz]
Sys.StrainPars = {'g(1)','g(2)','g(3)'};  % all three g principal values
Sys.StrainPars = {'A(2,3)'};              % Az of the second nucleus
Sys.StrainPars = {'g(3)','A(3)'};         % gz and Az
Sys.StrainPars = {'D(1)','D(2)'};         % D and E, with Sys.D = [D E]
Sys.StrainPars = {'J'};                   % exchange coupling between two electrons
Sys.StrainPars = {'gFrame(2)'};           % second Euler angle of the g tensor
StrainFWHM
array with one element per entry in StrainPars
Widths (FWHM) of the Gaussian distributions of the parameters, in the same order as in StrainPars and in the units of the parameters (e.g. MHz for A and D, radians for frame angles). 
Sys.StrainFWHM = 0.01;               % one parameter
Sys.StrainFWHM = [0.01 0.02 0.005];  % three parameters
Sys.StrainFWHM = [100 30];           % D and E, in MHz
Sys.StrainFWHM = 5*pi/180;           % frame angle, 5 degrees
StrainCorr
correlation matrix, or its upper triangle
Correlation coefficients between the parameter distributions, between -1 and +1. Give either the full symmetric matrix with ones on the diagonal, or only the elements above the diagonal in the order 1-2, 1-3, ..., 2-3, ... For two parameters, this is a single number. If StrainCorr is not given, the distributions are uncorrelated. StrainCorr can only be used together with StrainFWHM. StrainCorr is not well suited for fitting, since fitted values can combine into an invalid correlation matrix, which gives an error. For fitting correlations, use StrainModes. 
Sys.StrainCorr = -1;                             % two parameters, fully anticorrelated
Sys.StrainCorr = [0.5 0 0.3];                    % three parameters: 1-2, 1-3, 2-3
Sys.StrainCorr = [1 0.5 0; 0.5 1 0.3; 0 0.3 1];  % the same as a full matrix
Sys.StrainCorr = eye(4);                         % four parameters, uncorrelated
StrainModes
array with one row per mode and one column per entry in StrainPars
Alternative to StrainFWHM and StrainCorr. Each row describes one independent source of variation (a "mode") and lists, for each parameter, the FWHM by which it changes along this mode. The sign indicates whether the parameters change in the same or opposite direction. For example, StrainModes = [0.01 -20] for StrainPars = {'g(3)','A(3)'} describes a single mode where gz varies with a FWHM of 0.01 and Az with a FWHM of 20 MHz, in opposite directions (fully anticorrelated). The overall sign of a row does not matter. Several rows describe several independent modes. The modes do not need to be orthogonal or normalized. Only the overall distribution they describe matters, so different sets of modes that describe the same distribution give the same spectrum. This form is convenient for fitting correlated strains with esfit, since it always describes a valid distribution. 
Sys.StrainModes = [0.01 -20];          % one mode, two parameters, anticorrelated
Sys.StrainModes = [100 0; 0 30];       % two independent modes, same as StrainFWHM = [100 30]
Sys.StrainModes = [100 16.5; 0 28.58]; % two modes, correlated D and E

Examples: 

% Uncorrelated g strain along the three principal axes
Sys.g = [2.0 2.1 2.2];
Sys.StrainPars = {'g(1)','g(2)','g(3)'};
Sys.StrainFWHM = [0.01 0.02 0.03];

% Fully correlated g strain: gx and gy change together, gz in the opposite direction
Sys.g = [2.0 2.1 2.2];
Sys.StrainPars = {'g(1)','g(2)','g(3)'};
Sys.StrainModes = [+0.01 +0.02 -0.005];

% Anticorrelated gz/Az strain for a Cu(II) complex
Sys.g = [2.05 2.25];
Sys.Nucs = '63Cu';
Sys.A = [50 500];  % MHz
Sys.StrainPars = {'g(2)','A(2)'};
Sys.StrainFWHM = [0.02 30];
Sys.StrainCorr = -0.8;

% Correlated D and E strain for a triplet
Sys.S = 1;
Sys.D = [800 80];  % MHz
Sys.StrainPars = {'D(1)','D(2)'};
Sys.StrainFWHM = [100 33];
Sys.StrainCorr = 0.5;

Keep strain models simple. A model with many strained parameters and many correlation coefficients can reproduce almost any line shape, so there is a danger of overfitting: the fit to an experimental spectrum improves, but the strain parameters are poorly determined and lose their physical meaning. Typically, a strain model contains only a few parameters, which are either fully correlated (+1), fully anticorrelated (-1), or uncorrelated (0).

The strain model is a first-order approximation. It works best when the strains are small, so that the transition frequencies change approximately linearly over the range of parameter values. Some guidelines:

  • Keep the center of each distribution at least about 1.3 FWHM away from special values, such as D = 0, E/D = 1/3, a level crossing, or a sign change.
  • Angle strains should be small, a few degrees rather than tens of degrees.
  • Lines whose position is independent of a parameter to first order come out too narrow. An example is the central transition of half-integer spins S>1/2 with D strain, such as Mn(II) or Gd(III).
  • For degenerate energy levels, the widths depend on the choice of eigenvectors and are not reliable.

If in doubt, compare with an explicit average over the parameter distribution, as described below.

Strains are supported by pepper (with Opt.Method='matrix' or 'hybrid'), resfields, and resfreqs_matrix. They are currently not supported by the perturbation-theory functions resfields_perturb and resfreqs_perturb, nor by chili and saffron. salt ignores them.

Explicit averaging over distributions

You can model any form of inhomogeneous broadening. For this, run explicit loops over distributions of spin Hamiltonian parameters, simulate the associated spectra, and add them up, weighted by the distribution, to obtain a broadened spectrum.

This approach is necessary if the distributions are so broad as to render the built-in strain treatment invalid, or if you want to average over non-Gaussian distributions or over parameters that cannot be strained.

Here is an example for a broad Gaussian distribution of D: 

% Distribution of D
D0 = 1000;                           % center of distribution, MHz
Dfwhm = 500;                         % FWHM of distribution, MHz
D = D0 + linspace(-2,2,101)*Dfwhm;   % D values, covering +-2 FWHM around D0
weights = gaussian(D,D0,Dfwhm);
weights = weights/sum(weights);      % normalize weights to sum 1

% Spin system and experiment
Sys.S = 1;
Sys.HStrain = [30 30 30];            % MHz
Exp.mwFreq = 9.5;                    % GHz
Exp.Range = [230 430];               % mT

% Explicit average over the D distribution
spc = 0;
for k = 1:numel(D)
  Sys.D = D(k);
  [B,spc_] = pepper(Sys,Exp);
  spc = spc + weights(k)*spc_;
end

% Plotting
subplot(2,1,1);
plot(D,weights); axis tight; xlabel('D (MHz)');
subplot(2,1,2);
plot(B,spc); axis tight; xlabel('magnetic field (mT)');

Use enough parameter values: increase their number until the averaged spectrum no longer changes. As a rule of thumb, the lines of neighboring spectra in the loop should be shifted by less than the line width relative to each other.

If more than one parameter is distributed, then nested loops over a multi-dimensional distribution are needed.
